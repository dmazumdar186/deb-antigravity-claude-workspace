## wk 02 (posting 2026-11-02, with this film replacing the card image)

If you saw my post last week, then this continues in the same context; if not, this still addresses something valuable in my opinion.

To give a bit of context: a US outbound consultancy had a target of 800 cold emails a day across 32 inboxes. Around 24,000 a month. I had built the sending side. But I wanted to know what happened after someone replied (learned this as a product manager, learn what your customer does with your product because your next idea to provide more value sits there).

Because not every reply means the same thing.

For example: one person is out of office. Another wants to unsubscribe. Someone says no. Someone else is interested and wants to speak. And someone may ask a question that needs a proper answer.

And they all land in the inbox.

If the team has to open every message to work out which ones matter, the bottleneck has simply moved from sending to reading.

Worse, a genuinely interested prospect can sit there while the team works through everything else.

So I built the next part to handle the obvious replies automatically and bring the potentially valuable ones to the right person, with the context needed to follow up.

The CRM gets updated too, so the conversation doesn't disappear into someone's inbox.

You ask what do I mean? Well, an out-of-office reply is a good example. I don't need AI to recognise one. A simple rule catches it. I'd rather save the cost and use judgement where a real buyer may be involved.

So the learning is that the target was never 800 sends a day for its own sake. It was 30–60 positive conversations a month, with booked calls tracked in the CRM.

## wk 01 (already posted, for context; card: ref/wk01_card.webp)

A founder and his team at an SMB Acquisition firm were sending 15-20 cold emails a day. I eventually built a system that could send 24,000 a month.

So when I talk about AI, why I also tend to sound like I don't trust AI? Because the heart of problem solving still has been the same, and will always remain the same.

Before I started building, I asked them to show me the emails they were sending themselves and we curated that together, the messaging, the targeting, the offer...
Their emails were short, direct and usually ended with one question. No elaborate copy. And that was getting replies at around 3-4%.

The problem was simple. The could only send so many themselves.

I built the first version of the system to handle 24,000 emails a month. We are now slowly scaling it to 48,000 because the system behavior met its target goals.

But sending the emails was only part of the job.
When someone replied, the system had to know what to do next. Who should see it? How to classify the email responses as postive, neutral or negative? Should the sequence stop? What if something went wrong?
