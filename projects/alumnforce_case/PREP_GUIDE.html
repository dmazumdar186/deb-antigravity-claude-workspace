Part 0 — The 60-second version

If you read nothing else tonight, read this page. Everything here is unpacked, step by step and from zero, in Parts 1 to 10.

The company, in four lines

  • AlumnForce sells software to schools, universities and alumni associations so they can manage and stay in touch with their former students ("alumni"). See Part 1.
  • About 350 organisations each run their own separate copy ("instance") of the platform. An instance holds between 400 and 80,000 members and is run by one to three administrators. See Part 1 and Part 2.2.
  • The school pays; the alumni use it. That is "B2B2C": business to business to consumer.
  • Their technology: PHP/Symfony, Vue.js, MariaDB, OpenSearch, n8n, plus an internal "AI gateway" to a language-model provider. See Part 2.3.

What they asked

Administrators build mailing lists with a tool called the segmenter (about 60 filters, per the brief). It is slow, and only the experts use it. Leadership wants a campaign targeting assistant: the admin types "graduates working in finance in London for more than three years" and gets a usable list. Frame the product and propose the first increment for one quarter, with one PM and 3–4 shared developers. See Part 3.

What we found

The brief says speed. The twelve user quotes and the data file say trust. Eight of the twelve quotes are about whether the list is true, can be proven, or is safe to process, not about speed. The data file says 500 members but holds 525 rows (25 duplicates), spells London eight ways, and 68% of profiles have not been touched in two or more years. See Part 4.

The killer finding: in this file, people update their profile right after starting a job, then stop. So "in post for 3+ years" and "profile untouched for 3+ years" are the same signal. The brief's own example sentence returns exactly the people most likely to have moved on.

Say it like this: "Same sentence on your file: 2, 26 or 49 people, depending on how you clean it, and only 6 you can safely email."

What we propose

  • A reliability split on every list: Current / To confirm / To reactivate, each with an action (send, confirm, reactivate). This is the core of the increment. See Part 6.1.
  • Deterministic first, model second: cleaning, duplicate detection and the reliability status run in ordinary code, with no AI. The language model does one job only: it translates the admin's sentence into the segmenter's existing filters. See Part 6.3.
  • The human stays in control: the generated filters pre-fill the existing segmenter. The admin sees them, edits them and decides. The model never sends anything.
  • No member data goes to the model. Only the admin's question (with identifiers scrubbed) and the filter names leave the platform, to an EU-hosted model with no retention.
  • A sized quarter: a 26 dev-week core inside a 24–28 dev-week budget, with a pre-named cut, two feature flags off by default, and two stop dates (week 12 for the sentence box, week 17 for reactivation). See Parts 6.4–6.8.

Tomorrow

The CPO, in a 45-minute slot (16:30–17:15 Paris). Talk for about 15 minutes and leave the rest for conversation. Lead with the data line, the trust reframe and the stop criteria. See Part 10.

In one breath: They asked for a faster way to build lists. Their users' real problem is believing the list. So the first quarter ships a reliability layer that tells admins how far to trust each list, plus a narrow, evaluable sentence-to-filters translator that never sees member data.

1.1 What AlumnForce is, in one paragraph

AlumnForce is a French software company, based in Paris, that sells an online platform schools and alumni associations use to run their former-student community. The company calls it "la plateforme web & mobile simple et intuitive pour rassembler et engager votre communauté" (the simple, intuitive web and mobile platform to bring together and engage your community) (source). Inside one platform a school gets a member directory, news, email newsletters, events, dues and donations, a job board and career centre, mentoring and statistics (source). It was founded in 2008 by two brothers, Frédéric and Fabien Blanc (source). The legal company behind the product is called MEVIA, which owns the AlumnForce trademark (source; source). That is why your meeting invite comes from an @mevia.fr address.

Say it like this: "AlumnForce is the operating system of an alumni network: one place where a school finds its graduates, talks to them, brings them to events, collects dues and helps them with their careers."

1.2 Who the customers are, and who the users are

B2B2C

In plain words: "Business to business to consumer". AlumnForce (business 1) sells to a school or association (business 2), and the school offers the platform to its alumni (consumers). Think of a company that builds shopping-centre loyalty apps: the shopping centre pays, but the shoppers are the ones who use the app.

Why it matters here: Two audiences with different needs. The payer is the school, represented by one to three staff "admins" who run the platform. The users are the alumni, who never pay AlumnForce and may not even know its name. A feature must make the admin's job easier (they decide renewal) without annoying the alumni (whose activity is what makes the platform worth paying for).

Who pays (customers)

  • How many: the homepage says "plus de 350 réseaux" (more than 350 networks) (source). Our case brief says about 350 instances. These agree. An older profile of Frédéric Blanc says "more than 300 schools and universities in 15 countries" and "over 5 million users" (source, Unverified, undated).
  • What kind: business schools, engineering schools, universities, alumni associations, and also corporate alumni networks (former employees of a company). Named references on the site include HEC Alumni, Université Paris-Saclay, CentraleSupélec Alumni, EPFL, Sciences Po Alumni, Université de Strasbourg, ENSA Paris Val de Seine, BTP CFA Nouvelle-Aquitaine, and two corporate networks: Forvis Mazars and Adecco Group Alumni (source). Testimonials come from GEM Alumni and CentraleSupélec Alumni (source). Université de Rennes runs a platform on an alumnforce.org address (source).
  • How big: the site speaks of networks from 100 to 2,000 members up to communities of more than 20,000 (source). Our brief says 400 to 80,000 members per instance. Both can be true: the website talks about typical clients, the brief about the actual range.
  • Where: mostly France (French-language site, French references) plus Switzerland (EPFL) and Monaco (International University of Monaco is cited as a client by a data provider: source). The "15 countries" claim is Unverified.

Who uses it (end users)

  • Admins (school staff, alumni-association employees or volunteers): build the site, write newsletters, organise events, chase dues, pull lists. This is the person our case is about.
  • Alumni members: update their profile, search the directory, sign up for events, pay dues, browse jobs, find a mentor.
  • Students (on career and mentoring modules) and recruiters/companies (posting jobs, joining recruitment fairs) (source).

Multi-tenant, with isolated instances

In plain words: One software product, many separate copies of a client's data. Like an apartment building: same architecture and plumbing for everyone, but each family has its own locked flat. Each school is a "tenant" with its own "instance" (its own flat).

Why it matters here: A new feature can be switched on for one school and off for another (that is what "feature flags" are, see Part 2). And one school's data must never leak into another's. Detail in Part 2.

1.4 How they make money

SaaS subscription

In plain words: "Software as a Service". The client does not buy the software; it rents it each year, and AlumnForce hosts and updates it. Like paying for Netflix instead of buying DVDs.

Why it matters here: The money comes back only if the client renews each year. So what keeps a school renewing matters more than what makes it sign once.

Known: the price is a fixed setup fee plus an annual subscription based on the size of the database (number of members) (source). No public price list. Our brief adds that the business model for the AI feature (included, paid option or pay-per-use) is still open.

ARR, renewal and churn

In plain words: ARR (annual recurring revenue) is the total yearly subscription money from all clients, like the yearly rent a landlord collects from all flats. Renewal is a client signing up again for another year. Churn is the share of clients (or of revenue) that leave in a year, like tenants moving out.

Why it matters here: A schools business has long, slow sales cycles, so losing one client hurts. A feature that proves value to the admin's board (dues collected, time saved) protects renewal. That is the business reason behind our "trust" angle.

Part 2 — The technology, from zero

This part is a primer. It explains, from nothing, every technical word that appears in the case: in the deck, the annexes, the SQL and the speaker guide. Each concept gets a box with a plain-words explanation, an everyday analogy, and why it matters for AlumnForce. Read it once end to end. After that, a senior full-stack developer should be able to say any word from the case to you, and you will know what it means and why we used it.

One thing to hold on to from the start. AlumnForce imposed the technology stack. The brief says: Symfony/PHP, Vue.js, MariaDB, OpenSearch, n8n, and an internal AI gateway to a language-model provider (provider and region left open). We did not choose any of these. What we chose was to stay inside them and add nothing new. For each piece below, you will see a short table of alternatives so you can talk about the trade-offs. But the honest answer to "why MariaDB?" is always: "It was given. Staying inside it was our decision, and here is why."

Decision: stay inside the stack AlumnForce gave us

What we chose: Build everything with the tools already in the platform. No new database, no new framework, no new hosting. The reliability layer is one new MariaDB column and new fields in OpenSearch; the AI part is one call through their existing gateway.

Why: Three reasons. (1) The team is 3-4 developers shared with other projects; we plan on roughly half their time, 24-28 effective developer-weeks in the quarter. There is no room to learn a new tool. (2) No migration risk: moving data to a new system is slow and can break things for 350 tenants. (3) The brief requires the segmenter to work identically where the feature is off. The less we add, the easier that promise is to keep.

2.1 How a web app works

Before any of the stack makes sense, you need the basic shape of a web application. AlumnForce's admin tool, including the segmenter, is a web application.

Browser, server, database

In plain words: A web app has three parts. The browser (Chrome, Firefox) is what the admin looks at. The server is a computer somewhere else that does the work and decides what to show. The database is where the information is stored permanently. Think of a restaurant: the browser is the dining room and the menu, the server is the kitchen, the database is the pantry and the fridge.

Why it matters here: Our whole design is about where things happen. The reliability split is computed in the kitchen and pantry (server and database). The language model is a separate outside service the kitchen calls. Members' data never leaves the pantry.

Front end and back end

In plain words: The front end is everything that runs in the browser: buttons, forms, tables, the sentence box. The back end is everything that runs on the server: rules, calculations, talking to the database. Analogy: the front end is the waiter and the table setting; the back end is the cooks.

Why it matters here: At AlumnForce the front end is Vue.js and the back end is Symfony/PHP. When the deck says "Vue pre-fill", it means a front-end change: the segmenter screen fills itself with the filters. When it says "validator", that is back-end code.

Request and response

In plain words: Every time you click something, the browser sends a request ("give me members in London") and the server sends back a response (the list). Like ordering at a counter: you ask, you get a tray back.

Why it matters here: The demo promise is a response "in under 5 seconds". The deck also counts the language-model cost "per request". A timeout is when a response takes too long and the system gives up; our design then falls back to the normal segmenter.

API (application programming interface)

In plain words: An API is a menu that one program offers to another: a fixed list of things you may ask for, and the exact format of the answer. A program does not see the other's kitchen; it only orders from the menu. A power socket is a good analogy: any device with the right plug works, and you never need to know how the power station works.

Why it matters here: The AI gateway is reached through an API. The model provider is reached through an API. Our "filter contract" (Annex C) is in effect the menu we impose on the model: it may only answer in one format.

Endpoint

In plain words: An endpoint is one specific address on an API where you send a request. Like one specific counter in a post office.

Why it matters here: The deck says the sentence box launches only on an "EU-hosted endpoint", and the eval suite is "replayed nightly against the live endpoint". In both cases it means: the actual address that answers, in a known place.

Session, role, permissions

In plain words: A session is your logged-in visit: the system knows who you are until you log out. Your role (admin, editor, viewer) decides your permissions: which buttons and data you may use. Like a hotel key card that opens only certain doors.

Why it matters here: "The assistant runs inside the admin's session." So it can never see more than the admin can. The validator also removes any criterion the admin's role may not use, checked by unit tests (automated checks on small pieces of code).

Staging, production, CI

In plain words: Production is the real system clients use. Staging is a copy for rehearsals. CI (continuous integration) is an automatic robot that runs the tests every time a developer changes code. Analogy: a theatre's dress rehearsal (staging) before opening night (production), with a stage manager checking every prop each time (CI).

Why it matters here: The 80,000-member backfill is "measured on staging week 5" before any real tenant. The eval fixtures run "in CI" on every change.

In one breath: The browser shows, the server works, the database stores. Programs talk through APIs with fixed formats. Our design is about keeping member data in the database and server, and sending only a sentence out through an API.

2.2 SaaS, multi-tenancy and instances

SaaS (software as a service)

In plain words: Software you rent and use through a browser instead of installing it. The vendor runs it, updates it and bills a subscription. Like Netflix instead of buying DVDs.

Why it matters here: AlumnForce is a B2B2C SaaS: it sells to businesses (schools, universities, alumni associations) who use it to serve consumers (their alumni). So there are two kinds of "users": the admin (the client's staff) and the member (the alumnus). Our design keeps admin vocabulary ("to reactivate") away from members.

Tenant, single-tenant, multi-tenant

In plain words: A tenant is one client organisation. Multi-tenant means one software product serves many clients. It can be built two ways: everyone shares one big system with walls inside (like flats in one building), or each client gets their own separate copy (like separate houses on one street).

Why it matters here: AlumnForce serves about 350 tenants, each with "its own isolated instance". Instances range from 400 to 80,000 members, each run by one to three admins. So every change has to roll out to 350 houses, not one building.

Instance

In plain words: One running copy of the software with its own data. One house on the street.

Why it matters here: Isolation is a strength for privacy: one school's data cannot leak into another's. It also means work happens "per tenant": per-tenant reindex, per-tenant config, per-tenant cost line, per-tenant flag. Our cache key includes the tenant so nothing crosses between instances.

Per-tenant configuration

In plain words: Settings that each client can have differently, stored as data rather than written into the code. Like a thermostat in each house instead of one city-wide temperature.

Why it matters here: The reliability thresholds (2 years, 4 years, 1 year) are "a config value per tenant, not code", in a per-tenant assistant_config table with an owner and a change log. If hand-grading shows the thresholds are wrong, nobody has to redeploy software.

Feature flag

In plain words: An on/off switch for one feature, which can be flipped per client without new code. Like a light switch in each room.

Why it matters here: The brief says features activate "per instance or in bulk via feature flags". We use two flags, both off by default: reliability (status, criteria, counts) and nl_targeting (the sentence box). Two because a tenant may want the reliability split but refuse AI. With both off, "nothing changes in the segmenter".

Kill switch

In plain words: One master switch that turns a feature off everywhere at once, for emergencies. The big red button on a factory machine.

Why it matters here: A global kill switch sits at the gateway, owned by the Lead Developer. If the provider misbehaves, the sentence box disappears and the segmenter works exactly as today. That is "degraded mode": the feature is additive, so removing it breaks nothing.

Pilot, canary, waves

In plain words: Rolling out to a few first, then more. A canary is the first tiny group (named after the canary miners took underground: if it got sick, they left). Waves are the next, bigger batches. Like opening a new restaurant to friends, then one street, then the town.

Why it matters here: Pilot on ten tenants in weeks 8-12 (3 expert, 5 mid, 2 small, one refusing transfer). The reindex goes canary 1 → 3 → 10 tenants. After that, waves of about 50 tenants a month, at-risk renewals first; "350 tenants in about 7 months". A wave is "backfill plus flag, zero new development".

Batch job, nightly job

In plain words: A program that runs on its own on a schedule and processes many records at once, instead of reacting to a click. Like a cleaner who comes every night when the office is empty.

Why it matters here: The reliability status is a "nightly tag": every night it recomputes, for each member, whether the profile is current, to confirm, or to reactivate. Annex D adds the safety rails: a per-tenant lock (two runs never collide), a watermark (it remembers where it stopped and resumes), and a dead-man alarm if any enabled tenant's status is older than 36 hours.

Backfill

In plain words: Filling in a new field for all the existing records, once, when the field is created. If you add a "date of birth" box to a form, the backfill fills it in for everyone who signed up before.

Why it matters here: When a tenant switches on, we must compute normalised values and statuses for all its existing members. It is "chunked" (done in slices so the system is not overloaded) and runs off-peak. The largest instance, 80,000 members, must finish in one night; that is measured on staging before any wave.

Idempotent

In plain words: Running it twice gives the same result as running it once. A lift button: pressing "3" five times still takes you to floor 3 once.

Why it matters here: Nightly jobs fail and get re-run. If the backfill were not idempotent, a re-run could double-count or corrupt data. Ours "upserts on member_id" (update if it exists, insert if not) with a run id, and the plan proves idempotency "by a double run on staging before wave 1".

Additive change, migration

In plain words: An additive change only adds new things and leaves old ones untouched, like adding a shelf. A migration changes the existing structure of stored data, like rebuilding the shelves while books are on them. Migrations are riskier.

Why it matters here: Q1 is deliberately additive: new *_norm columns that "never overwrite source fields", new index fields, no new criterion type, no new operator. The relative-date operator is a real engine migration, so it is pushed to Q2 with an "expand/contract" migration (add new, move over, then remove old) rehearsed on a staging snapshot.

Metering, quota, rate limit

In plain words: Metering counts use per client, like an electricity meter. A quota is a monthly allowance. A rate limit caps how fast one person can make requests, like a turnstile.

Why it matters here: The brief says every model call costs money and AlumnForce must know which client generated it. Every call writes a log row: tenant, pseudonymised admin, prompt hash, redacted sentence, tokens in/out, cost, model, region, latency. Fair use is 300 requests a month per tenant, then a soft block and a CS alert.

In one breath: AlumnForce runs 350 separate instances, so everything is per tenant: flags, config, reindex, cost. We roll out with switches, small groups first, and nightly jobs that are safe to re-run.

MariaDB (the main database)

Relational database, tables, rows, columns

Tables can point to each other (a member row points to a degree row). MariaDB is a free relational database, a close cousin of MySQL.

Why it matters here: Member records live in MariaDB. The reliability status is "one MariaDB column". The normalised values go in new *_norm columns next to the originals.

SQL and a query

In plain words: SQL is the language you use to ask a relational database questions. A query is one question. For example: "SELECT members WHERE city is London AND job started before 2023-10-12." It reads almost like English. Analogy: a very precise librarian who answers only questions in a fixed grammar.

Why it matters here: Annex B is SQL. It deduplicates the CSV, normalises city, country and sector, computes the reliability status, then selects the London finance members. Index (in a database)

In plain words: A sorted shortcut that lets the database find rows without reading every one. Like the index at the back of a book.

Why it matters here: Mostly a developer concern; it explains why adding new searchable fields has a cost. (Do not confuse it with an OpenSearch "index", below, which means a whole searchable collection.)

NoSQL and document stores (e.g. MongoDB)

In plain words: A family of databases that do not use fixed tables. A document store like MongoDB keeps each record as a flexible "document" (a bundle of fields, which can differ from one record to the next). Analogy: a relational database is a filing cabinet with printed forms where every box must be in the same place; a document store is a folder of free-form notes, each shaped as you like.

Why it matters here: Member records are regular, structured and linked (member, degree, job, dues). Admins filter on exact fields with AND/OR. That is what SQL is best at, and it gives strict rules that keep data consistent. A document store would add flexibility we do not need and lose consistency we do. And it was not ours to choose.

OpenSearch (the search engine)

Search engine and inverted index

In plain words: A search engine is a second copy of the data, organised for very fast searching and counting. OpenSearch is an open-source search engine (a fork of Elasticsearch).

Why it matters here: The segmenter runs its filters on MariaDB/OpenSearch. To make the reliability status filterable and countable fast, it must be in OpenSearch too.

Index, document, field, mapping (in OpenSearch)

In plain words: In OpenSearch, an index is a whole searchable collection (all members of one tenant, say). Each member is a document. Each property is a field. The mapping is the declared type of each field: date, number, text, or keyword (an exact label, not split into words). Analogy: the mapping is the form template that says "this box holds a date".

Why it matters here: D1 adds two date fields (profile last updated, last login) and a reliability_status keyword to the mapping. Because they are dates, the two new criteria can reuse the segmenter's existing date filter: "a mapping update and a reindex, not an engine migration."

Aggregation (terms aggregation)

In plain words: Asking the search engine to count, not list. A terms aggregation says "group results by this label and count each group". Like counting votes by candidate.

Why it matters here: The split (e.g. "72 members: 31 current, 14 to confirm, 27 to reactivate", illustrative) is "one terms aggregation" on the status keyword. Cheap and fast.

Reindex and alias swap

In plain words: When you change the mapping, you usually rebuild the index: reindex. To do it without downtime, you build the new index next to the old one, check it, then flip an alias (a nickname the app uses, like "members-current") from old to new in one instant. Like building a new bridge beside the old one and switching traffic only when it is tested. Rollback is flipping the alias back.

Why it matters here: Each tenant needs a reindex. Annex D: "build new index, verify counts, flip alias; canary 1 → 3 → 10 tenants; rollback = alias flip." If an 80,000-member reindex will not finish in one night, nothing is user-visible: the alias flips only once the new index is verified.

2.4 Data quality concepts

The case's central finding is that the data, not the filter, is the problem. These are the words used to describe and fix it. (The findings themselves are discussed in Part 4; here we explain the vocabulary.)

CSV and a dataset

In plain words: A CSV ("comma-separated values") is a plain text spreadsheet: one line per row, values separated by commas. A dataset is any collection of such records.

Why it matters here: The case file profils-exercice-PM.csv was announced as 500 members and holds 525 rows. Its column names are French (ville = city, pays = country, secteur_declare = declared sector, entreprise = employer, poste = job title, date_debut_poste = job start date, date_maj_profil = profile update date, derniere_connexion = last login, promo = class year).

Deduplication

In plain words: Finding records that are the same person entered twice, and keeping one. Like noticing "J. SMITH, gmail" and "John Smith, work email" in your phone are the same contact.

Why it matters here: Rows M0501 to M0525 are 25 apparent duplicates: surname in capitals or compound, truncated first name, gmail address, same job and start date. The Annex B rule (same first three letters of first name, one surname contained in the other, same job start date) drops exactly 25. A looser "or same job" rule wrongly merged Raphaël Martin into Raphaël Martinez. Hence: in production, a scored candidate list with blocking keys (cheap first filters that decide which pairs are worth comparing), reviewed by the admin, never an automatic merge.

Normalisation

In plain words: Turning many ways of writing the same thing into one standard form. "london", "LONDON", "Londres", "London - Canary Wharf" all become "London". Like sorting a messy drawer so all the spoons are in one place.

Why it matters here: London is written 8 ways in the file; the UK 3 ways, and 41 members have no country. Without normalisation the same sentence finds 2 members; with it, 26. Normalised values go in *_norm columns and never overwrite what the member typed.

Geocoding and a radius filter

In plain words: Geocoding turns a place name into map coordinates (latitude, longitude). Then you can ask "within 30 km of London" instead of matching spellings. Like placing a pin on a map and drawing a circle.

Why it matters here: The segmenter already supports "within 200 km of Lyon", so a geocoder exists. Our validator uses it to resolve the model's place_text locally: "In London maps to a geocoded radius, not to eight spellings." A "radius cap" sanity bound stops absurd circles.

Careful: Annex B's SQL uses spelling patterns plus a country gate (to exclude London, Ontario), because the CSV has no coordinates. The product uses the geocoder. If a developer asks, both are true: SQL for the proof on the file, geocoder for production.

Taxonomy, closed list

In plain words: A taxonomy is an agreed list of categories. A closed list means only values from that list are allowed. Like a multiple-choice question instead of a free-text answer.

Why it matters here: The model may only output values from closed taxonomies: sector list, country list, diploma types. It cannot invent "Fintech-ish". Free text (places, employers) is resolved locally, not by the model.

Reference tables and overrides

In plain words: A reference table is a lookup list, such as "employer → sector" (Goldman Sachs → finance). A per-tenant override lets one client correct an entry for itself. Like a shared dictionary where each school can pencil in its own notes.

Why it matters here: 70 of 127 people at banks declare "Services", "Other" or nothing as their sector. An employer table catches them. Annex D: when several tenants confirm the same override, it is promoted to the global table: "350 instances teach one table."

Data freshness and the reliability status

In plain words: How recent and therefore how likely to be true a record is. Like the "best before" date on food.

Why it matters here: This is the heart of the product. Each member gets one of three statuses: Current (profile updated within 2 years, or login within 1 year), To confirm (updated 2 to 4 years ago), To reactivate (older, or no date). Future-dated logins (60 in the file, up to October 2027) are ignored so they never count as recent. In the file, 68% of profiles are untouched for 2+ years.

Reference date and the "as of" date

In plain words: The reference date is the "today" a calculation uses. The "as of" date is stamped on a result to say when it was true. Like a bank statement "balance as of 30 September".

Why it matters here: Annex B uses reference date 2026-10-12, so "3+ years" means job started on or before 2023-10-12. A saved segment shows "Computed on 15/10/2026 · relative dates recalculated when opened or sent" so Karim can quote it to his board.

Relative dates vs absolute dates

In plain words: An absolute date is fixed ("1 January 2024"). A relative date moves with time ("3 years ago"). A saved list built on "3 years ago" but stored as a fixed date quietly goes out of date, like a note saying "meet me tomorrow" found a week later.

Why it matters here: The segmenter has no relative dates today. In Q1 the segment stores {relative: {years: 3}, resolved_at} and the shared list loader re-resolves it when opened or sent. If the admin edits the date by hand, it becomes "fixed date". A native relative-date operator is a Q2 engine change.

Proxy (signal standing in for another)

In plain words: When you cannot measure what you want, you use something related. Like judging a restaurant by its queue.

Why it matters here: "In London for 3+ years" cannot be checked: there is no date for the location, only for the job. Job tenure is the proxy, labelled "3+ years in current role" in the UI. Also: profile updates cluster just after a job start (median 112 days), so in this file "in post 3+ years" and "profile untouched 3+ years" are the same signal.

Data integrity defect

In plain words: Data that is impossible, not just old: a login date in the future. Like a receipt dated next year.

Why it matters here: The 60 future logins are routed to "a data-integrity ticket owned by the data owner" and ignored by the status.

In one breath: The file has duplicates, eight Londons and missing sectors. We fix it deterministically (dedup, normalise, geocode, reference tables) and stamp every member with a freshness status, so every count carries how far to trust it.

2.5 AI and LLM concepts

LLM (large language model)

In plain words: A program trained on huge amounts of text that predicts the most likely next words. It can follow instructions, summarise, translate. It does not "know" your database; it only sees what you send it. Analogy: a brilliant, fast translator who has read everything but has never been to your office.

Why it matters here: We use the LLM for one job only: translate the admin's sentence into a structured filter set. "The model is a translator with a fixed vocabulary, not a search engine over people."

Provider and model version

In plain words: The provider is the company that runs the model (and its hosting). A model version is one specific release. Providers sometimes update a model behind the same name without warning, like a supplier changing a recipe but keeping the label.

Why it matters here: "Providers update silently." So we pin the model id, log the provider version on every call, and replay the evals nightly against the live endpoint (2.6).

Prompt

In plain words: The full text you send to the model: instructions plus input. Like the brief you give a freelancer.

Why it matters here: Our prompt contains: the scrubbed sentence, the tenant's criteria schema (names and operators only), and closed taxonomies. Never a member row, never an employer list. The "prompt version" is pinned and logged.

Tokens, input vs output, cost per token

In plain words: Models read and write in tokens, chunks of a few characters (roughly three-quarters of a word in English). Providers bill per token. Input tokens are what you send; output tokens are what the model writes back; output usually costs more per token. Like a taxi charging per kilometre, with a higher rate on the way back.

Why it matters here: One request is about 1,500 to 2,500 input tokens and 150 to 300 output tokens: "a fraction of a euro cent per request on a mid-tier model, under one euro a month for a heavy tenant." These are order-of-magnitude estimates, to be confirmed on the gateway in week 1. "The cost that matters is engineering": 26-28 developer-weeks.

Prompt caching, cached prefix

In plain words: If the start of every prompt is identical (the prefix), the provider can remember it and charge less and answer faster next time. Like a pharmacist who keeps your regular prescription on file and only needs today's change.

Why it matters here: The schema and taxonomies are the cached prefix, so only the short sentence changes per request. This is why we send the full tenant schema and strip by role afterwards: a role-pruned schema would break the shared prefix.

Response cache and cache key

In plain words: Separately from the provider's prefix cache, we can store a whole answer and reuse it if the exact same question comes again. The cache key is the label that decides "is this the same question?". Like a coat-check ticket: the ticket must match exactly.

Why it matters here: Annex C: key = hash of the scrubbed sentence + tenant + taxonomy hash + schema hash + prompt version + model id + role; kept 24 hours. Tenant and role in the key mean no answer leaks across instances or roles. A hash is a short fingerprint of a text. A taxonomy change bumps its hash and purges the cache.

Structured output, JSON, JSON schema

In plain words: JSON is a standard text format for data with labelled fields, readable by any program. A JSON schema is the rulebook saying which fields must exist and what type each is. Structured output means forcing the model to answer in that exact shape instead of prose. Like a tax form with fixed boxes instead of a letter.

Why it matters here: The "filter contract" (Annex C) is a JSON shape: a list of filters, each with criterion, op, values (or place_text, relative), plus an unmapped list. "Schema in, JSON out" is also why the provider is swappable in days.

Validator

In plain words: Ordinary code that checks the model's answer before it is used. A customs officer at the border: nothing enters without inspection.

Why it matters here: Our validator rejects unknown criteria, strips criteria outside the admin's role, geocodes places, resolves durations, maps free values to reference tables, applies sanity bounds (radius cap, value types, a result-count versus tenant-size alert), and logs tenant, admin, tokens and cost. It is deterministic, so it is testable.

Retry, fallback, timeout, "cannot map"

In plain words: A retry asks again if the answer is broken. A fallback is the safe plan B. Like redialling once if the line drops, then leaving a voicemail.

Why it matters here: "One repair retry then cannot map." "Cannot map" is the honest answer when a sentence cannot be expressed with the segmenter's criteria ("finance or consulting unless Paris": the segmenter has no nested groups). Mapped parts still apply; the unmapped part is shown inline: "Add this criterion by hand", never a blank page. Hard timeout → degraded mode, segmenter unchanged.

Temperature

In plain words: A dial for how adventurous the model's wording is. At 0 it picks the most likely answer every time.

Why it matters here: Annex C pins temperature 0, so evals are as repeatable as possible.

Hallucination

In plain words: When a model states something false with confidence, or invents a value. Like a guest who answers every question rather than admit they do not know.

Why it matters here: Our design limits the damage: closed lists, a validator that rejects unknowns, a "cannot map" path, and filters always visible to the admin with the count before any send. A valid-but-wrong filter set is caught by the human reading it.

Prompt injection

In plain words: Text crafted to hijack the model's instructions ("ignore the rules and select everyone"). Like slipping a fake memo into a stack of orders.

Why it matters here: The worst case is a rejected or visibly wrong filter set (for example widened to everyone), which the admin sees with its count before any send. The model never sends, merges or edits. "Widening attacks" are in the negative eval set.

Scrubbing, redaction, placeholders

In plain words: Removing or masking personal details from text before it leaves. A placeholder swaps a name for a token like {{P1}} and swaps it back afterwards, like blacking out names on a document and keeping a key to restore them.

Why it matters here: Pre-flight scrubs e-mails, phones and exact full-name matches from the tenant's member table. Other capitalised two-word sequences ("Goldman Sachs") become placeholders, re-substituted locally so "those who work at Goldman Sachs" still maps. We log hits and never block, because surnames like Paris, Martin or Finance would block constantly.

Deterministic vs probabilistic

In plain words: Deterministic code gives the same output for the same input, every time: a calculator. A model is probabilistic: it can vary. Like the difference between a vending machine and a human barista.

Why it matters here: "Deterministic first, model second." Dedup, normalisation and the status run in MariaDB/PHP with no model call. Tenants who refuse AI still get all of it.

EU region, hosting, zero retention, no training

In plain words: The region is where the provider's computers physically run. Zero retention means the provider keeps no copy of your prompts. No training means your prompts are never used to improve their model. Like a courier contract saying: parcels stay in the EU, no photocopies, no reading.

Why it matters here: These are three of the words in the DPO sentence: "to a model hosted in the EU under a no-retention, no-training contract." Note "US-owned EU region" is a distinct case: the legal entity and transfer mechanism must still be named (2.7).

Embeddings, semantic search, RAG

In plain words: An embedding turns a text into a list of numbers so that similar meanings sit close together. Semantic search uses that to find "meaning matches", not just word matches. RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) means: search your data first, then paste what you found into the prompt so the model can answer from it. Like giving the translator a stack of relevant files before asking a question.

Why it matters here: Excluded from Q1. Embedding eight spellings of London "embeds the noise"; it is only useful after normalisation; and RAG sends member text to a model, a new DPO question. "Q2 at the earliest."

Fine-tuning vs prompting

In plain words: Prompting means instructing an off-the-shelf model each time. Fine-tuning means further training a model on your own examples so it changes permanently. Briefing a temp worker each morning versus sending a permanent hire on a training course.

Why it matters here: Excluded, along with self-hosting: "No evidence it is needed; prompt plus schema plus evals is enough and keeps the provider swappable. Revisit with eval data."

LangChain and orchestration frameworks

In plain words: Libraries for chaining many model calls, tools and steps into a pipeline or "graph". Useful for complex multi-step agents. Analogy: a full factory conveyor system when you only need one machine.

Why it matters here: "One call with a JSON schema and a deterministic validator is a function, not a graph." Keep the disciplines (typed schemas, tracing, evals in CI), not the framework. Reconsider if calls are chained in Q2.

Agent

In plain words: A model set up to take actions (not just answer), possibly over several steps.

Why it matters here: Only in the 2027 vision: "An agent proposes a dues campaign to the right 180 people... and waits for a human yes." Not in Q1: the model never acts.

2.6 Measuring AI

Evals

In plain words: Automated exams for the model: a list of inputs with the correct answer, scored every time something changes. Like a driving test you re-take after every car modification.

Why it matters here: Re-run on every prompt, schema or model change. Failure blocks release (PM blocks, Lead Developer notified).

Golden set, labelled sentences

In plain words: The golden set is the trusted answer key: real example sentences, each labelled with the correct filter set by humans.

Why it matters here: 100 real sentences from tickets and verbatims, built with CS in weeks 1-2. Edited sentences in production become new candidates, so the set grows.

Annotators and Cohen's kappa

In plain words: Annotators are the people who write the labels. Two label independently. Cohen's kappa measures how much they agree beyond what chance alone would give: 1 is perfect, 0 is chance. Like checking two referees would have given the same calls.

Why it matters here: Target kappa at least 0.8. If two humans cannot agree on the right answer, the model cannot be graded on it.

Exact match (canonicalised), precision, recall

In plain words: Exact match: the model's whole filter set equals the label. Canonicalised means both are put in a standard order first (criteria sorted, values sorted), so order differences do not count as errors. Precision: of what the model gave, how much was right. Recall: of what was right, how much the model found.

Why it matters here: Gate: exact match at least 85%, with its interval stated; zero unknown criteria or values after validation. Precision and recall are reported per (criterion, operator, values) tuple.

Negative set

In plain words: Trick questions the model must refuse. Like testing that a vending machine rejects fake coins.

Why it matters here: 30 sentences that must return "cannot map": impossible, discriminatory, injection, widening attacks. Gate: negative-set recall at least 95%.

Confidence interval

In plain words: The honest margin of error on a measured number. A poll saying "52%, plus or minus 3".

Why it matters here: On about 100-130 items, a score like 85% carries roughly ±7 points of noise (95% interval). So an 86% does not prove we are above 85%, and we say so.

Careful: The sources attach "±7 points" both to "100 items" (slide 10) and to "130 items" (the 100 labelled + 30 negative, Briefing section 7). The gate on exact match runs on the 100 positive sentences; the nightly replay uses all 130 fixtures. If asked, say: "roughly ±7 points on about a hundred items."

Record/replay and fixtures

In plain words: Record the model's real answers once, save them as fixtures, then replay them in tests without calling the model again. Fast, free and repeatable, like rehearsing with a recording of the orchestra.

Why it matters here: CI runs on recorded fixtures; live runs happen on a prompt or model change (k=3 runs, flake budget 2%).

Nightly live replay and drift

In plain words: Drift is quality slowly changing without anyone touching your code, because the provider changed something. A nightly live replay re-asks the real model every night to catch it. A smoke detector, not a one-off inspection.

Why it matters here: Alert on a 5-point drop; release blocked, provider version compared, prompt pinned.

Edit rate, rejection rate, cannot-map share

In plain words: Signals from real use. Edit rate: how often admins change the generated filters before saving. Rejection rate: typed a sentence, saved nothing. Cannot-map share: how often the model honestly gave up.

Why it matters here: Stop criterion at week 12 includes "edit rate stays above 50%". Cannot-map fragments, ranked, become the criteria roadmap. All aggregate, pseudonymised, and disclosed to admins at flag-on.

Baseline

In plain words: The "before" measurement, so the "after" means something.

Why it matters here: Weeks 1-4 measure time-to-list, exports and tickets on the current segmenter. Every threshold is "an initial guess, reset on the week-4 baseline".

In one breath: 100 labelled sentences plus 30 trick ones, two annotators who must agree (kappa ≥ 0.8), exact match ≥ 85% with ±7 points stated, negatives refused ≥ 95%, replayed every night to catch silent provider changes, then edit rate in real use.

2.7 Privacy and law (EU/GDPR) basics

Not legal advice; the vocabulary you need. The deck states legal bases as assumptions or questions, not facts.

Personal data and GDPR

In plain words: Personal data is any information about an identifiable person: name, e-mail, job, city. GDPR is the EU law that governs how it may be used: for a purpose, with a legal basis, minimised, kept secure, not kept forever.

Why it matters here: Clients are mostly European higher-education institutions. The brief: data sent to a model must be "justified, minimised and traceable", and some clients will refuse any transfer. The deck answers with four mechanisms: "minimised, justified, traceable, refusable".

Controller, processor, sub-processor

In plain words: The controller decides why and how data is used (the school). The processor handles it on their behalf (AlumnForce). A sub-processor is a processor's own supplier (the model provider). Like a homeowner, a builder, and the builder's electrician.

Why it matters here: "Tenant controller, AlumnForce processor, model provider a sub-processor with 30-day notice" (clients are told 30 days before a new sub-processor is added).

DPA, DPO, DPIA

In plain words: DPA (data processing agreement): the contract between controller and processor, or processor and sub-processor. DPO (data protection officer): the person in an organisation who advises on and checks GDPR compliance; they can say no. DPIA (data protection impact assessment): a written risk study before a risky processing starts, like a safety inspection before opening a building.

Why it matters here: Philippe's DPO will say no to a US model unless told precisely what leaves. The DPA with the provider must say zero retention, no training. AlumnForce supplies a DPIA screening memo and a balancing-test template; each tenant records its own decision.

Legal basis and legitimate interest (balancing test)

In plain words: GDPR requires a reason in law for processing. Legitimate interest is one: the organisation has a real interest, weighed in a balancing test against the person's rights.

Why it matters here: The "likely basis" for the reliability status is the tenant's legitimate interest in accurate records: "the tenant's call", not ours.

Data minimisation

In plain words: Use and send only the data you truly need. Pack for the trip you are taking, not every trip you might take.

Why it matters here: Only the scrubbed sentence, criterion and operator names, and closed taxonomy values leave. Tenant-defined labels go as typed ids ("custom_12") unless the tenant opts in.

Pseudonymisation

In plain words: Replacing a direct identifier with a code, keeping the key elsewhere. It is still personal data, but safer. Like patient numbers instead of names on lab samples.

Why it matters here: Logs store a pseudonymised admin, kept 12 months, exportable per tenant, deleted on offboarding.

Transfer to the US

In plain words: Sending EU personal data to a country outside the EU needs a legal mechanism. A US-owned company can also be subject to US law even when its servers are in the EU, which is why the legal entity matters, not only the server location.

Why it matters here: Hosting row in Annex C: "EU-hosted endpoint; provider legal entity and jurisdiction named; transfer mechanism stated if US-owned."

Derived personal data and profiling

In plain words: New information you compute about a person from their data is personal data too. Scoring people is a form of profiling.

Why it matters here: "Reliability status is derived personal data." So it is computed per tenant only after the flag is on, never for all 350 by default; it is shown to the member on their profile.

Right to contest

In plain words: A person can challenge data about them.

Why it matters here: Member sees "Profile last updated 4 years ago · Confirm / Correct". A contest is answered within 10 business days and freezes the status as "confirmed" for 12 months (relief, never a downgrade).

Retention, suppression, opt-out

In plain words: Retention is how long data is kept. Opt-out is a person saying "stop contacting me". Suppression is the do-not-contact list that enforces it.

Why it matters here: Logs: 12 months. One reactivation per member per quarter. A "not me" reply or opt-out means suppression. Deleting stale profiles stays the tenant's own policy.

Security questionnaire

In plain words: The standard list of security questions large clients send before buying (encryption, certifications, hosting, incident response).

Why it matters here: Annex C has one row per item (TLS, i.e. encrypted connections; gateway auth; certifications; residency; pentest scope, i.e. paid hacking tests; incident SLA) "so Sales never answers 'it's secure'".

Say it like this: "Only the administrator's question, scrubbed of identifiers and full names, and the names of the filters leave the platform, to a model hosted in the EU under a no-retention, no-training contract. No member profile is ever sent; residual hits in the question are logged and reviewed."
In one breath: The school is the controller and decides; AlumnForce processes; the provider is a sub-processor under a no-retention, no-training DPA in the EU. The status is derived personal data, so it is opt-in per tenant, visible and contestable.

4.3 The data audit, step by step

We opened the CSV and profiled it column by column. A CSV is a spreadsheet saved as plain text, one row per member, values separated by commas (Part 2). Step 1: 525 rows, not 500

The brief announces 500 members. The file holds 525 rows (526 lines including the header). The extra 25 rows have IDs M0501 to M0525. All 25 are duplicates of people already in the file.

How we found them. The IDs above M0500 were the first clue. Comparing each one with the rest showed a pattern: the surname in capitals or compound, a shortened first name (for example "Man."), a gmail address, the same job and the same job start date. Example from VERIFICATION.md: M0148 Camille Roux and M0502 Camille ROUX. Same job (M&A Associate at Amundi), same start date (6 December 2015), and a gmail address on the second record.

The matching rule (Annex B, step 0). Two rows are the same person when all three hold:

  1. the first three letters of the first name match;
  2. one surname is contained in the other, after removing capitals, hyphens, spaces and dots;
  3. the job start date is identical.

When a pair is found, we keep the most recently updated record. This rule drops exactly the 25 duplicates and leaves 500, which matches the brief.

Decision: require the same job start date to call two rows the same person

What we chose: name match and identical job start date.

Why: an earlier version said "same name pattern or same job". It merged Martin into Martinez, two different people, because "martin" is contained in "martinez". That gave 26 drops and 499 members, one person lost. A start date is very specific (day, month, year), so two strangers rarely share it. With it, exactly 25 rows drop and 500 remain. The Annex B result stays 49 either way.

4.4 The key insight: three years in post means three years without an update

This finding makes the case specific to AlumnForce, not a generic "data is dirty" slide.

We compared each member's last profile update with their current job start date. Updates cluster just after a job start. The median gap is 112 days: half of members updated within 112 days of starting the job, half later. The interquartile range is 50 to 165 days: the middle half of members updated between 50 and 165 days after the start. Only 2 % of updates come before the job start. After that window, updates stop.

Median and interquartile range

In plain words: line everyone up from fastest to slowest updater. The median is the person in the middle. The interquartile range is the span covering the middle half of the line, leaving out the fastest quarter and the slowest quarter. Unlike an average, a few extreme cases cannot drag these numbers around.

Why it matters here: "112 days, 50 to 165" says the pattern is tight. Most people update within months of a new job, then go quiet.

How we read it: people update their profile when they change jobs, then go silent. It is a correlation in this file, not a proven cause, and the deck says so.

The consequence: in this data, "in post for 3+ years" and "profile untouched for 3+ years" are the same signal. The brief's own sentence ("for more than three years") selects exactly the people whose profiles are most likely out of date. All 43 "To reactivate" members in the Annex B result have a profile older than 3.9 years and no login in two years.

That is Nathalie's disaster, reproduced on their file. A naive assistant answering her request returns precisely the people most likely to have moved.

Say it like this: "In this file, three years in post and three years without a profile update are the same signal. The people your sentence asks for are the ones to reactivate first."

Decision: freshness thresholds of 2 years and 4 years (plus 1 year for logins)

What we chose: Current = profile updated less than 2 years ago, OR a login less than 1 year ago (future dates ignored). To confirm = profile updated 2 to 4 years ago. To reactivate = older, or no date at all.

Why: with updates landing a few months after a job change, a profile under 2 years old probably reflects the current job. Between 2 and 4 years, the person may have moved. Over 4 years, assume they have. A recent login is separate proof the person is still there. The values are explicitly initial guesses: in weeks 1 and 2, Customer Success grades 100 members by hand on 2 tenants, and the rule must agree at least 80 % of the time before the nightly job ships.

5.1 The design principles, and where each one comes from

A design principle is a rule you decide before you design, so that later choices are quick and consistent. Think of it as the house rules you agree before a renovation: "we keep the load-bearing walls", "we never touch the wiring without an electrician". Each principle below is traced to a finding from Part 4.

Principle 1: trust first

What we chose: the first thing the platform returns is not a list but how far to trust the list. Every segment comes back split into Current, To confirm and To reactivate (the rules are in Part 6.1).

Why: three verbatims are about truth (Nathalie's "London expats" dues call to 340 people where twenty replied they had moved back years ago and two unsubscribed; Julie's "Analyst at Lehman Brothers"; Inès's "nine list tickets in ten are a wrong profile, not a wrong filter"). 6.1 The product principle: a reliability split on every segment

Every segment the admin builds, whether by typing a sentence or by hand, comes back with three counts. Picture a fruit stall where every crate has a label: "picked today", "check before selling", "ask the farmer". The fruit is the same; the label tells you what to do with it.

The exact rules

StatusRule (initial thresholds)What the admin does

The bounds are "half-open": Current is [0, 2 years) since update or [0, 1 year) since login; To confirm is [2, 4 years); everything else, including a missing date, is To reactivate. The 60 future-dated logins found in the file are ignored so they can never count as "recent".

Where the thresholds come from: they are initial guesses anchored on the finding that profile updates come a median 112 days after a job start (interquartile 50 to 165 days). They are checked in weeks 1 to 2: Customer Success (CS) hand-grades 100 members on 2 tenants, and the rule must agree with them at least 80 % of the time before the nightly job ships. They are stored as a per-tenant setting, not hard-coded, so moving them costs nothing.

Assumption: 2 years / 4 years / 1 year are the right thresholds. If wrong: hand-grading shows it in weeks 1–2; the values change in the per-tenant config table before anything ships. On this synthetic file the split is lopsided (43 of 49 to reactivate), which is itself a reason to test.

Why these words

"Grade" and "stale" read like a verdict on how well the client looks after its data; "chat" sets a ChatGPT expectation. "To reactivate" is an action, not a reproach. There are only two names in the product: the tab is Trusted Segments, the chips are Reliability: Current / To confirm / To reactivate.

Careful: never say "chat", "grade" or "stale" in the room. Production labels will be French UI strings, decided with CS in week 1, under the same rule: an action word for the third status, never a verdict.

What the member sees: admin vocabulary never reaches members. On their own profile they see "Profile last updated 4 years ago · Confirm / Correct": one button, no label.

Decision: a reliability split, not a chatbot

6.3 The architecture: how one sentence is processed

"Orchestration" means the order in which the pieces run and who hands what to whom, like a kitchen's ticket system: the waiter takes the order, the chef cooks, the pass checks the plate (see Part 2). Our core idea: the model is a translator with a fixed vocabulary, not a search engine over people. Three actors appear below: the human (the admin), deterministic code (AlumnForce's own PHP, MariaDB and OpenSearch; rules that always give the same answer), and the model (the language model, reached only through AlumnForce's internal AI gateway, a single controlled door to the provider).

Stack choices AlumnForce imposed

In plain words: Symfony/PHP (the server code), Vue.js (the screens), MariaDB (a SQL database: tables with rows and columns, like a strict spreadsheet), OpenSearch (a fast search index, like a book's index), n8n (a workflow tool) and the internal AI gateway were all given by the brief. We did not pick them.

Why it matters here: staying inside the stack means no new technology for 3–4 shared developers to learn and patch. Alternatives such as a NoSQL database or a separate AI service were never on the table; see Part 2 for what they are.

Worked example: the brief's own sentence through the pipeline

Take "find me the graduates working in finance in London for more than three years". This is a teaching walk-through built from Annex B and Annex C; the exact model output is illustrative.

  1. Flag on, quota fine. Scrub finds no e-mail, phone or member name. "London" and "finance" are allow-listed place and sector words, so no placeholder.
  2. The model returns roughly: sector in the finance values of the closed sector list; city near "London"; experience start before {years: 3}. "Graduates" maps to everyone (class years 2005–2024 in the file).
  3. The validator geocodes "London" into a radius and, per Annex B, a country gate keeps London, Ontario out. "3 years" is stored as relative and resolved to an absolute date (on the reference date 2026-10-12, a job start on or before 2023-10-12).
  4. The deterministic layer has already done the heavy lifting overnight: the 25 duplicates removed (500 members), eight London spellings and three UK spellings normalised, finance read from three sources in order (declared sector, then an employer reference list, then job-title keywords; in the product the NAF industry code comes first and keyword matches are shown "to confirm").
  5. Result on the file: 500 → 168 in London → 72 in finance → 49 with 3+ years in post. Sources: 24 declared, 17 employer, 8 keyword (3 of the 8 doubtful).
  6. Split: 1 current, 5 to confirm, 43 to reactivate. So "6 to e-mail today". All 43 have a profile older than 3.9 years and no login in two years.

Compare the naive filter on the file as delivered (city value "Londres", sector value "Finance", 3+ years in post): 2 members (3 without the tenure clause). Normalised, no dedup: 26. This 2 → 26 → 49 → 6 cascade is the proof that the deterministic layer, not the model, decides whether the answer is true.

Why "3 years" is a trap in this data

In plain words: people update their profile about 112 days (median) after starting a job, then go silent. So "in this job for 3+ years" mostly means "nobody has checked this profile for 3+ years". It is like asking a library for books "on loan for 3+ years": you get exactly the ones most likely lost.

Why it matters here: the very sentence in the brief selects the least reliable members. Also, the data has no date for location, only for the job, so the UI labels it "3+ years in current role".

Dedup in production (not the Annex B rule)

The Annex B rule (same first-name prefix, surname contained, same start date) is a one-off for a 525-row file. In production: blocking keys first (name prefix plus class year, so you only compare likely pairs instead of every pair among 80,000), then a scored candidate list inside each block (name similarity, same start date, same employer), shown to the admin with the score. Never auto-merge. Thresholds tuned on pilot tenants' confirmed and rejected merges. This is the Q2 duplicate report and merge UI.

6.8 Measurement

Offline evals: the gate before any tenant sees it

An "eval" is a test exam for the model (see Part 2).

  • Golden set: 100 real sentences from tickets and verbatims, labelled by two people independently; agreement measured with Cohen's kappa (a score of how much two labellers agree beyond chance) of at least 0.8.
  • Negative set: 30 sentences that must return "cannot map" (impossible, discriminatory, injection, widening attacks).
  • Gate: canonicalised exact match (sorted criteria and values equal to the label) of at least 85 %, with its roughly ±7-point interval stated; negative-set recall at least 95 %; zero unknown criteria or values after validation. Role stripping is covered by unit tests, not evals.
  • Pinned: model id, temperature 0, prompt version; live runs k=3, flake budget 2 %.
  • Re-run on every prompt, schema or model change; replayed nightly against the live endpoint with an alert on a 5-point drop, because providers update silently.

Online metrics

Edit rate (filters changed before saving, per segment), rejection rate (typed, nothing saved, per sentence), cannot-map share. All per tenant, aggregate and pseudonymised, disclosed to admins at flag-on. Weekly sample of 20 sentences, never attributed to a person. Adoption: share of new segments via the sentence box, weekly active admins, return use in weeks 2 and 4, Excel export share (should fall), list tickets per 100 campaigns (target −30 % on pilot tenants).

Value metrics

Time from first click to saved segment (baseline weeks 1–4); "not me / I moved" replies and unsubscribes per campaign; share of current profiles month over month; profile updates from reactivation; dues response of current + to-confirm sends versus historical.

Part 7 — Assumptions, and what moves if each is wrong

Every plan rests on guesses. A good plan says which parts are guesses, how you will check them, and what you will change if a guess turns out wrong. This part lists every assumption the case makes. For each one you get four things: what we assumed, why that was a reasonable guess, how and when we test it, and what moves if it is wrong. The "what moves" answers come from the sensitivity list in our briefing (section 5). Learn the shape, not the words: "we assumed X, here is why, we check it in week N, and if it is wrong, Y changes and Z does not".

Say it like this: "Every number in this plan is an initial guess with a test date. I'd rather show you where I'm guessing than pretend I'm not."

One rule runs through the whole part: the reliability tag and the metering never move. Almost every assumption below, if wrong, changes the sentence box, the timing or the price. None of them removes the reliability layer. That is deliberate: it is the part with value on its own.
